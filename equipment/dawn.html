01

Overview

Ion propulsion is slow but efficient: Dawn's engines pushed with a force like a sheet of paper, yet over years they changed its speed by many kilometres per second. That let one spacecraft leave an orbit and enter another, something chemical rockets could not do on that budget.

Dawn was NASA’s first truly interplanetary spaceship. The mission featured extended stays at two very different extraterrestrial bodies: giant asteroid Vesta and dwarf planet Ceres. Both small worlds reside in the debris-strewn main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

Dawn launched in 2007 on a journey that put about 4.3 billion miles (6.9 billion kilometers) on its odometer. Propelled by ion engines, the spacecraft achieved many firsts until its extended mission concluded on Oct. 31, 2018. The Dawn mission was designed to study two large bodies in the asteroid belt in order to answer questions about the formation of the Solar System, as well as to test the performance of its ion thrusters in deep space. Ceres and Vesta were chosen as two contrasting protoplanets, the first one apparently "wet" (i.e. icy and cold) and the other "dry" (i.e. rocky). The Dawn mission ran out of fuel in 2018 and still orbits the dwarf planet Ceres.

02

Where it began

Launched in 2007, Dawn used an ion engine, a gentle but efficient thruster that fires charged xenon atoms and allowed a single spacecraft to visit two worlds.

03

The mission

Dawn reached the asteroid Vesta in 2011, left in 2012, and arrived at dwarf planet Ceres in March 2015.

04

How it worked

Cameras, a mapping spectrometer and a gamma-ray detector mapped the surface and mineral composition of both worlds.

05

What it found

Ceres has bright salt deposits in Occator Crater, signs of briny water that rose to the surface. Vesta looks like a planetary building block frozen in time.

06

Why it was left behind

Dawn ran out of hydrazine, the fuel for its steering thrusters. Without it, the craft could not point its antenna at Earth or its panels at the Sun.

07

Where it is now

Dawn orbits Ceres in a path that will not crash for at least 20 years, so Ceres is left with its own tiny artificial moon.

At Vesta, Dawn found a heavily cratered world with a huge basin and showed it is a source of many meteorites found on Earth. At Ceres it found bright salt spots in Occator Crater and a mountain called Ahuna Mons, likely a cryovolcano. These results show Ceres had liquid under its crust.
